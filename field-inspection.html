Overview

Permit software built for 1998 was running cities in 2023.

Municipal permitting is one of the last industries still running on legacy desktop software — think table-heavy interfaces, fax-based submissions, and zero cross-department visibility. Cityworks had been selling the same core product for 14 years. Churn was accelerating as competitors offered browser-based tools, and the sales team kept losing deals to a single objection: the dashboard.

I was brought in to redesign Permit Flow from the ground up — a new product layer that would sit on top of the existing data model and give permit managers, inspectors, and city administrators a unified view of everything in flight.

Permit Flow — product overview
Problem

Nobody knew where a permit stood. Nobody.

The existing system showed permits as rows in a table sorted by submission date. There was no status grouping, no escalation visibility, no inspection scheduling view. If a permit was held up by a missing document, a code review flag, and an inspection backlog simultaneously — you had to open three different screens and do the math yourself.

The deeper problem: no shared language for status

After 12 discovery sessions across 8 cities, every department had invented its own status taxonomy. Engineering used "In Review," "Flagged," and "Approved." Planning used "Active," "On Hold," and "Pending Revisions." Inspections used numeric codes. The redesign wasn't just a UI problem — it required creating a unified status model that could map to every department's existing mental model.

Discovery synthesis — status taxonomy mapping
Approach

Four principles that kept the design honest.

Municipal software users aren't power users — they're clerks, inspectors, and administrators with 20-tab workloads and zero patience for learning curves. Every decision had to survive contact with someone who just wants to close tickets and go home.

01
Status first, always

Every permit should communicate its lifecycle position and bottleneck in under three seconds. Dense tables were replaced with a stage-and-urgency layout that surfaces the right signal without clicking.

02
Escalation before volume

A permit stuck for 30 days is more important than one submitted today. The dashboard surfaces risk as a primary signal — not a filter you have to remember to apply.

03
One source of truth

Eliminate the morning spreadsheet. Every status update, document request, and inspection note lives in the system and is visible to every stakeholder without a phone call.

04
Roles that reflect reality

A permit clerk and a city administrator have fundamentally different relationships to the same permit. Role-based views surface the right information without duplicate data entry or separate logins.

Early concepts — workflow board explorations
Design

From status table to lifecycle command center.

The core redesign moved the primary view from a flat submission list to a lifecycle board organized by stage and hold reason. Each permit card shows the minimum viable information to make a routing or escalation decision — all detail lives one level deeper.

Lifecycle board — primary view

The unified status model

I designed a six-stage status model — Submitted, In Review, Action Required, Scheduled, Approved, Issued — that mapped cleanly onto every department's existing taxonomy. "Action Required" was the key addition: a cross-department hold state that bypassed departmental code confusion and surfaced the permit to the responsible party immediately.

Status model — mapping to department taxonomies

Contractor-facing status portal

Fifty percent of inbound calls were applicants asking for status. I designed a lightweight public-facing portal — no login, just a permit number — that showed exactly where an application stood and what was needed next. This alone reduced inbound call volume by 34% in the pilot cities.

Public status portal — applicant view
Retrospective

What worked. What we'd do differently.

What went well
  • The unified status model became the product's most referenced feature in sales calls — it solved a problem competitors hadn't named yet
  • Grounding the status taxonomy in observed workarounds (Karen's morning spreadsheet) produced requirements no stakeholder had articulated
  • The public portal was a 3-week build that delivered one of the highest-impact outcomes of the entire project
What we'd do differently
  • We underestimated the data migration effort — legacy data didn't map cleanly to the new status model, adding 6 weeks to rollout per client
  • Mobile wasn't scoped until field inspectors raised it. They were an afterthought until they weren't
  • The aggregate analytics layer was deprioritized and would have dramatically increased executive buy-in at launch
Reflection

What I took away.

01

The workaround is the design brief.

Karen's morning spreadsheet wasn't a quirk — it was a product spec. Every manual workaround a user has built is a signal that the software has failed them. The most useful thing I did in discovery was ask to see how people actually managed their work, not how they wished they could.

02

Naming is design work.

The unified status model was as much a language design problem as a UX one. "Action Required" had to mean the same thing to an engineering reviewer, a permit clerk, and an applicant checking their portal. Getting that right required more alignment sessions than interface iterations.

03

Look for leverage outside the core product.

The public portal was a 3-week side project that outperformed every metric the core dashboard hit in its first 90 days. The highest-impact design decisions don't always live inside the product's main interface.
