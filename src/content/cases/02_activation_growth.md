## Customers were getting stuck before the real work began.

Part3 helps architects coordinate construction administration, but getting a new project ready required several setup decisions before users could begin working.

Customer Experience was repeatedly helping firms configure teams, assign reviewers, and understand settings that didn't match how their offices worked.

The challenge wasn't simply reducing the number of onboarding steps. It was understanding which steps helped customers reach value and which ones were getting in the way.

[STATIC — Customer journey showing setup friction, meaningful work, and the point at which collaborators become involved.]

## Three opportunities across one journey

Rather than treating onboarding as a single wizard, I worked across the moments where customers needed to start, repeat, and delegate work.

**Start without unnecessary setup.**

Project creation needed a clearer path for firms starting from scratch or using an existing configuration.

[STATIC — Project creation choices.]

**Stop configuring the same work twice.**

Architecture firms often repeat naming conventions, document settings, and workflows across projects. I designed the template experience to make that configuration reusable instead of asking people to rebuild it every time.

[HERO ANIMATION — Configure a project template → Save → Create another project → Reuse the settings.]

**Make it easier to bring the team in.**

Projects depend on multiple people, companies, and disciplines. Adding them individually introduced friction and opportunities for mistakes.

The member-import experience let users add people in bulk, review their details, and confirm the team before proceeding.

[ANIMATION — Import members → Review companies and roles → Confirm team.]

## Measuring activation more meaningfully

The work also exposed a measurement problem: the team needed a clearer definition of when a customer had actually begun using Part3.

A historical cohort analysis established a **37% end-to-end activation baseline** under a defined 180-day measurement window.

That was a starting point for evaluation, not evidence that these changes caused an improvement.

The larger lesson was that activation couldn't be reduced to finishing setup. We needed to understand whether customers started meaningful work, brought collaborators into it, and had reasons to return.

Why this works: The case is about identifying and prioritizing friction across a customer journey. The reusable-template flow demonstrates design craft, while the activation measurement shows product thinking.

I'd keep the 37% number explicitly labeled as a baseline. It belongs near the top because defining the measurement was meaningful work, not because it is an improvement metric.
