## 01. Project Files

Flagship case · 0→1 product · Approximately 3-minute read

# We weren't building another file browser.

**Turning a file-management brief into a new paid product for managing construction drawings.**

**58K+ drawings managed · 0→1 paid product launched**

**Role:** Led end-to-end product design, from information architecture and interaction design to launch support and growth.

[HERO — Full-width drawing register with realistic sheets, current revisions, and issue references.]

## A different problem was hiding inside the brief.

Project Files began as a straightforward request: give architects somewhere to organize and upload their files.

But architects already had places to store PDFs. What they couldn't easily manage was the information changing inside those files. Drawings were revised, reissued, and shared throughout construction, often across multiple tools.

Working with product and engineering, I helped shift the direction from generic file management to drawing management.

The important distinction was simple: **a PDF is a file, but a drawing has an identity and history.**

[STATIC COMPARISON — Early file-browser concept beside the structured drawing model. Highlight sheet number, current revision, and issuance source.]

## Making a drawing set manageable

Construction teams often receive hundreds of sheets inside a combined PDF. Asking architects to split, name, and organize every page manually would make the product feel like more work than the tools they already used.

I designed an import experience that extracts individual sheets and their details, then lets users review the results and correct mistakes before publishing.

The tradeoff was between automation and control. We wanted to remove repetitive setup without asking architects to trust every extracted value.

[ANIMATION — Upload combined PDF → Extract sheets → Review identified drawings → Correct an exception → Publish.]

## Keeping the current drawing connected to its history

Version control became more complicated once drawings were connected to the rest of construction administration.

A sheet needed a stable identity across revisions, but the same identifier could appear in different drawing packages. Teams also needed to understand which document had issued an update.

I designed the revision experience around those relationships rather than treating every upload as a separate file.

[HERO ANIMATION — A-201 → Issue revised sheet through Site Instruction 3.0 → A-201 Rev 4 becomes current.]

[STATIC CLOSE-UP — Current revision, previous revisions, issue date, and issuing document.]

The result was a drawing register that could show what was current without losing the record of how it changed.

## From product launch to product adoption

Project Files launched as a paid product area, with customers now managing **58K+ drawings** in Part3.

The structured drawing model also gave the team a foundation for revision comparison, drawing references, and specifications.

But launch raised a second challenge. Uploads and purchases showed demand; they didn't tell us whether teams were regularly using drawings throughout construction.

That distinction continues to shape how I think about the product: storing the latest information is only the starting point. The value comes from making it useful in the work people do every day.

Why this works: It shows 0→1 judgment, domain complexity, detailed interaction design, and a real product outcome without making the reader go through a chronological design-process recap.

The strongest visual should be your A-201 revision interaction. Import supports the story, but the revision model is what makes the product distinctive.

## 02. Activation & Growth

Growth and cross-functional leadership · Approximately 2–3-minute read

This case should be more evidence-led than Project Files. We shouldn't make three product improvements appear to be three independently proven growth wins.

# Getting started wasn't the same as getting value.

**Rethinking activation across project setup, reusable workflows, and team onboarding.**

**37% historical activation baseline established · Three connected onboarding opportunities addressed**

**Role:** Product design and activation prioritization, working with product, engineering, and Customer Experience.

[HERO — Strong composition showing project creation, reusable templates, and team onboarding. Three connected views, not three identical screenshots.]

## Customers were getting stuck before the real work began.

Part3 helps architects coordinate construction administration, but getting a new project ready required several setup decisions before users could begin working.

Customer Experience was repeatedly helping firms configure teams, assign reviewers, and understand settings that didn't match how their offices worked.

The challenge wasn't simply reducing the number of onboarding steps. It was understanding which steps helped customers reach value and which ones were getting in the way.

[STATIC — Customer journey showing setup friction, meaningful work, and the point at which collaborators become involved.]

## Three opportunities across one journey

Rather than treating onboarding as a single wizard, I worked across the moments where customers needed to start, repeat, and delegate work.

**Start without unnecessary setup.**

Project creation needed a clearer path for firms starting from scratch or using an existing configuration.

[STATIC — Project creation choices.]

**Stop configuring the same work twice.**

Architecture firms often repeat naming conventions, document settings, and workflows across projects. I designed the template experience to make that configuration reusable instead of asking people to rebuild it every time.

[HERO ANIMATION — Configure a project template → Save → Create another project → Reuse the settings.]

**Make it easier to bring the team in.**

Projects depend on multiple people, companies, and disciplines. Adding them individually introduced friction and opportunities for mistakes.

The member-import experience let users add people in bulk, review their details, and confirm the team before proceeding.

[ANIMATION — Import members → Review companies and roles → Confirm team.]

## Measuring activation more meaningfully

The work also exposed a measurement problem: the team needed a clearer definition of when a customer had actually begun using Part3.

A historical cohort analysis established a **37% end-to-end activation baseline** under a defined 180-day measurement window.

That was a starting point for evaluation, not evidence that these changes caused an improvement.

The larger lesson was that activation couldn't be reduced to finishing setup. We needed to understand whether customers started meaningful work, brought collaborators into it, and had reasons to return.

Why this works: The case is about identifying and prioritizing friction across a customer journey. The reusable-template flow demonstrates design craft, while the activation measurement shows product thinking.

I'd keep the 37% number explicitly labeled as a baseline. It belongs near the top because defining the measurement was meaningful work, not because it is an improvement metric.

## 03. Construction Data & AI

Product vision and AI interaction design · Approximately 2–3-minute read

This one needs a different treatment. There are no verified customer outcomes yet, so the case should lead with what you proposed and what became tangible, not manufacture a success metric.

# What if reporting didn't require knowing what to ask?

**Exploring a visual-first approach to construction analytics, with AI available when users need to go deeper.**

**Outcome: Product direction proposed to leadership · Internal dashboard builder demonstrated**

**Role:** Product concept and interaction design exploration. Engineering developed the internal dashboard implementation.

[HERO — Full-width dashboard with meaningful construction metrics, charts, and a contextual AI panel.]

## Powerful data was still difficult to use.

Part3 already captured information about submittals, RFIs, reviews, and project activity. But turning that information into useful reporting wasn't straightforward.

Advanced integrations and AI tools offered new possibilities, but they also assumed users knew what information to request.

I proposed a different starting point: give users a useful view of their data first, then let them ask questions or build on it.

[STATIC — Dashboard overview, highlighting useful default metrics and the contextual Ask entry point.]

## Start with something people can understand.

Not every user wants to describe a report in a prompt.

I explored a dashboard experience where project teams could browse suggested charts, choose what to measure, and adjust the grouping or time period.

The interface makes those choices visible instead of hiding them inside a conversation.

[ANIMATION — Browse charts → Configure measure and grouping → Add to dashboard.]

## Use AI to investigate, not replace the evidence.

Once someone spots a trend, the next question is often why it happened.

I explored contextual questions directly from a chart, allowing users to examine a change and follow the answer back to the underlying project records.

The important distinction was between helping users interpret a question and giving them a result they could verify.

[HERO ANIMATION — Select review-time trend → Ask why it increased → View breakdown → Open source record.]

[STATIC CLOSE-UP — AI answer beside its supporting records.]

## Let people create without learning a reporting tool.

Natural language offered another way to create charts. Rather than generating a final dashboard without review, the proposed interaction let users inspect the chart's scope and destination before adding it.

[STATIC — Chat-generated chart preview, showing metric, grouping, date range, and confirmation.]

The dashboard direction was presented to leadership and followed by an internal engineering preview. The implementation uses Part3's existing data APIs, with deterministic metrics rather than AI-generated numbers.

The product was still in development at this stage, so customer adoption and effectiveness remain unmeasured.

**The opportunity:** Make more of Part3's existing data useful to the people managing projects, without requiring them to become reporting experts.

Why this works: The story demonstrates product vision through an actual interaction model. It avoids presenting AI as the achievement in itself.

I would make one final accuracy check before publishing: distinguish your proposed Figma interactions from the parts engineering actually implemented. The notes support your dashboard concept and Alex's subsequent internal build, but not a public launch.

## 04. Self-Serve Billing

Monetization and permissions · Approximately 2-minute read

Billing should be the shortest case. It has one excellent central tension and doesn't need to be inflated into a broad transformation story.

# Access shouldn't wait for a billing decision.

**Designing provisional access for teams that need to move quickly without losing control over paid seats.**

**44 firms used self-serve seat management · 245 seat-change events recorded**

**Role:** Product design for member access, billing approval, and related user states within a larger cross-functional initiative.

[HERO — Provisional-access employee state paired with the billing-admin approval experience.]

## The person adding a teammate wasn't always the person paying.

Previously, expanding a team's paid seats required help from Part3 staff. That could interrupt onboarding just as a firm was growing its use of the product.

But self-serve purchasing introduced another complication. An organization administrator might be responsible for adding people while a principal or billing administrator controlled spending.

Making those two people complete the same action wasn't realistic.

## Two reasonable approaches, one important tradeoff

An approval-first model protected spending control but left the new member waiting.

The alternative was to grant the requested access immediately and give the billing administrator a limited window to approve it.

The team adopted the second approach, with a seven-day approval period and a fallback to Viewer access if approval didn't arrive.

[STATIC — Approve-first versus provision-first comparison, showing the consequences for the employee and the billing administrator.]

## Making temporary access understandable

The experience needed to work for both sides.

A new member had to know their access was temporary without feeling blocked from starting work. The billing administrator needed to understand the pending request and what approving it meant.

The design also had to account for requests that expired without a decision.

[HERO ANIMATION — Provisional access → Pending request → Billing-admin approval → Confirmed access.]

[STATIC — Approved versus expired states, including the Viewer fallback.]

## What changed

The broader self-serve billing system recorded **44 firms taking seat actions and 245 seat-change events** by the October 2026 audit.

Approvals were more common than direct seat purchases. That was an important finding: much of the customer need was about approving access that someone else had already requested, not simply buying additional seats.

The usage figures cover the wider self-serve seat-management system, not the isolated effect of the provisional-access design.

The design challenge was balancing two legitimate needs: letting people get to work and giving organizations control over spending. Provisional access gave those needs separate paths without treating either as an afterthought.

Why this works: One clear product decision, two different user roles, a meaningful edge case, and real system-level usage evidence.

The source material attributes the overarching billing specification and engineering leadership to Corey, so the draft deliberately doesn't claim you originated provision-first. Before publishing, the role description should be matched to the design decisions you can personally substantiate.
